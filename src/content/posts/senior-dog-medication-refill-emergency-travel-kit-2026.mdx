## 1. Start with the refill calendar, not the suitcase

A senior dog that depends on daily medication needs a refill plan before travel, storm season, boarding, or caregiver handoff. Count actual doses on hand, note refill lead time, and ask the clinic how early they can approve refills. Do not assume a destination pharmacy, emergency clinic, or online service can replace a missing prescription during a trip.

Keep the travel-medication note practical: drug name as prescribed, refill date, veterinarian contact, pharmacy contact, storage need, and when to call the clinic. Do not publish prescription labels, microchip numbers, addresses, or medical records.

## 2. Ask the clinic what must travel with the dog

Request a current medication list, dose schedule, diagnosis summary when appropriate, vaccination record, microchip number, emergency contact, and any travel certificates required for the destination. Keep private details protected, but make sure a caregiver can show a veterinarian what the dog takes and when.

## 3. Pack medication so it can still be used safely

Use original or clinic-approved containers when possible, protect medicine from heat, moisture, crushing, and accidental access, and ask the veterinarian or pharmacist about temperature requirements. Do not split pills, change dose timing, or combine products in a new container unless the clinic says it is safe.

Keep the travel-medication note practical: drug name as prescribed, refill date, veterinarian contact, pharmacy contact, storage need, and when to call the clinic. Do not publish prescription labels, microchip numbers, addresses, or medical records.

## 4. Separate routine comfort from emergency action

A travel kit should include leash, harness, food transition notes, water, bowls, cleanup bags, towel, medication instructions, and clinic contacts. Emergency action is different: breathing trouble, collapse, repeated vomiting, seizure, suspected overdose, or severe pain should trigger veterinary or emergency-clinic contact rather than home troubleshooting.

Keep the travel-medication note practical: drug name as prescribed, refill date, veterinarian contact, pharmacy contact, storage need, and when to call the clinic. Do not publish prescription labels, microchip numbers, addresses, or medical records.

## 5. Hand off the plan without exposing private data

If a sitter or family member helps, give the smallest useful set of information: dog name, weight if relevant, medication schedule, allergies, vet contact, emergency permission boundaries, and where records are stored. Do not put payment cards, account passwords, or unnecessary owner medical details in the kit.

## One-page checklist

- Confirm the current official or expert source before acting.
- Write the owner, deadline, and next review date.
- Keep private identifiers, passwords, serial numbers, payment data, medical details, and sensitive screenshots out of shared notes.
- Use a temporary workaround only if it does not create a larger safety or money risk.
- Escalate to the relevant professional when symptoms, account compromise, safety hazards, legal questions, or large financial commitments are involved.

## FAQ

### Does this replace professional advice?
No. It is a pet-care planning guide. Use your veterinarian, emergency clinic, pharmacy, airline/travel carrier, and official import or disaster guidance when medication, travel, or emergency decisions are high-stakes.

### Why include records and privacy limits?
Good records help the next person act, but over-sharing creates new risk. The useful middle is a private, minimal note that documents the decision without exposing credentials, account data, payment details, medical details, private footage, or serial numbers.
