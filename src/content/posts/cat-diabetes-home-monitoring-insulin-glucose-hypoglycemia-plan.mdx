Feline diabetes home care is not a do-it-yourself dosing project. It is a **measurement, observation, and communication system** built around a veterinarian’s diagnosis and written treatment plan. The goal is to notice meaningful change without reacting to every number, prevent medication mix-ups, and know when symptoms make waiting unsafe. Cornell’s [feline diabetes overview](https://www.vet.cornell.edu/departments-centers-and-institutes/cornell-feline-health-center/health-information/feline-health-topics/feline-diabetes) describes common signs such as increased thirst and urination, weight loss, and appetite changes, but those signs also occur with other diseases. Home notes can support a diagnosis and follow-up; they cannot establish one.

This guide is educational and does not prescribe insulin, oral diabetes drugs, food, glucose targets, or monitoring frequency. The current [AAHA feline diabetes guideline](https://www.aaha.org/resources/2026-aaha-diabetes-management-guidelines-for-cats/) and its [peer-reviewed full text](https://jaaha.kglmeridian.com/view/journals/aaha/62/3/article-p65.xml) emphasize individualized management. Use the clinic’s instructions over any example below, especially for a missed dose, vomiting, reduced appetite, suspected hypoglycemia, ketones, or a cat receiving an SGLT2 inhibitor.

## Build the written plan before a stressful morning

Ask the veterinarian to put the operational details in writing. Record the medication name, concentration, device, prescribed amount, route, meal relationship, planned check times, refill lead time, and the clinic’s after-hours contact. Also request explicit instructions for four common disruptions: the cat eats less than expected, vomits, a dose may have been missed, or two caregivers are unsure whether a dose was already given. Never improvise by doubling, splitting, repeating, or withholding a dose unless the treating veterinarian’s plan says to do that in the exact situation.

Use one active medication station and one log. Retire old labels, old syringes, and superseded instructions from the active area so they cannot compete with the current plan. If two cats receive medicines, separate their supplies physically. A caregiver should write “given” only after administration, with actual time, rather than checking a box in advance. This is especially important when work schedules, travel, illness, or multiple caregivers change the normal routine.

The PubMed record for the [2026 AAHA guideline](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42014100/) is useful for verifying the guideline’s publication details. It does not turn a general recommendation into a dose for an individual cat. The clinic must interpret the cat’s examination, laboratory results, diet, concurrent disease, and response.

## Use a compact daily log, not a wall of numbers

A useful log lets the veterinarian reconstruct context. Include:

| Field | What to record | Why it helps |
|---|---|---|
| Medication | name, prescribed amount, actual time, initials | exposes missed or duplicate-dose uncertainty |
| Food | offered food and approximate completion | puts readings and symptoms in context |
| Water and urine | change from the cat’s normal pattern | flags trends without pretending to diagnose |
| Weight | same scale and similar conditions, at the clinic’s interval | shows directional change better than memory |
| Behavior | energy, hiding, gait, interaction, grooming | captures functional change |
| Vomiting or diarrhea | time, frequency, food/medication relationship | supports same-day triage |
| Glucose or sensor event | value, units, device, time, meal and dose timing | prevents a number from losing context |

Do not create false precision. “Drank much more than usual and emptied the bowl by afternoon” may be more honest than inventing ounces. For urine, note litter-clump frequency and relative size only if that method is consistent and does not disturb the cat. PetWellHub’s litter comparison and trial guide explains how to change one box variable without confusing a medical observation. Thirst, urination, appetite, and weight are cross-condition observations rather than a home diagnosis.

## Keep insulin-treated and oral-drug pathways separate

Do not assume every diabetic cat has the same treatment or emergency profile. The FDA’s approval notice for [bexagliflozin](https://www.fda.gov/animal-veterinary/cvm-updates/fda-approves-first-oral-treatment-cats-diabetes-mellitus), together with the peer-reviewed SGLT2 evidence below, describes oral SGLT2-inhibitor options for specific newly diagnosed cats that meet eligibility requirements. These drugs are not interchangeable with insulin, and their labeling includes important selection and monitoring conditions.

A peer-reviewed review of [SGLT2 inhibitors in feline diabetes](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10816666/) discusses both their role and ketoacidosis concerns. A cat receiving an SGLT2 inhibitor may develop serious ketoacidosis even when glucose is not dramatically high. That means a household rule such as “the glucose is not high, so we can wait” is unsafe. Follow the product-specific and veterinarian-specific sick-day and ketone plan.

For insulin, timing, food intake, and duplicate-dose uncertainty are operational priorities. For an oral SGLT2 drug, eligibility, appetite, hydration, clinical signs, and ketone-related instructions are crucial. Keep the exact treatment pathway at the top of the log so a substitute caregiver or emergency clinic does not infer the wrong one.

## Understand glucose tools without worshipping the display

Veterinarians may use clinic curves, home meters, or continuous glucose monitors. A [review of feline diabetes diagnosis and management](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12612538/) explains why clinical signs, fructosamine, glucose data, and treatment response must be interpreted together. Stress can increase feline glucose, while home devices and human-calibrated meters can differ from veterinary laboratory methods.

A [study of continuous glucose monitoring in diabetic cats](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10832725/) supports the usefulness of trend data while also illustrating practical limitations. Sensors can detach, compress, lag behind blood glucose, produce gaps, or disagree with the cat’s condition. Treat a surprising sensor value as a prompt to assess the cat and follow the clinic’s confirmation plan, not as automatic permission to change medication.

If a clinic uses both mg/dL and mmol/L, the mathematical conversion is:

- mg/dL ÷ 18 = mmol/L
- mmol/L × 18 = mg/dL

For example, 180 mg/dL is 10 mmol/L, and 5 mmol/L is 90 mg/dL. This conversion does **not** create a target or treatment threshold. Preserve the original unit and device in the log because transcription and unit errors can be dangerous.

## Create a three-level escalation card

The treating clinic should define exact thresholds. The household can still organize actions into a plain-language card:

### Emergency now

Seek urgent veterinary direction for collapse, seizure, profound weakness, inability to stand, severe disorientation, unresponsiveness, breathing difficulty, repeated vomiting with marked illness, or any symptom the clinic identified as possible hypoglycemia or ketoacidosis. Do not force food or liquid into an unconscious or poorly responsive cat. Do not drive while simultaneously trying to monitor or treat the cat; secure transport and call ahead when possible.

### Same-day veterinary call

Call the clinic the same day for reduced appetite, a meal refused near medication time, vomiting, new marked lethargy, worsening thirst or urination, unexplained weight change, a sensor repeatedly showing unexpected results, possible missed or duplicate medication, ketone concern, or uncertainty about the written plan. “Same day” is not a promise that the situation is nonurgent; the clinic may redirect to emergency care.

### Record for scheduled review

Stable trends such as gradual changes in appetite, water intake, litter habits, activity, or weight belong in the log and should be shared at the clinic’s chosen interval. A trend is more useful when collection is consistent and the cat is not stressed by constant testing.

The household should never create a universal glucose cutoff from an internet article. Device type, sampling error, clinical signs, treatment, and the veterinarian’s targets all matter. When the cat looks wrong but the number looks reassuring, the cat’s condition wins.

## Reduce routine stress so observations are usable

Home monitoring can fail when every interaction becomes restraint. Keep feeding, sampling, and medication areas quiet and predictable. Use rewards approved for the treatment plan, allow the cat to approach, and stop a nonurgent attempt if restraint is escalating fear or injury risk. If someone else may provide care, the [emergency foster handoff guide](/posts/pet-emergency-foster-care-handoff-plan-2026/) shows how to record medication and escalation boundaries without asking a helper to interpret the disease.

Weigh only at the veterinarian’s recommended frequency. Use the same scale and surface, and avoid interpreting a tiny single change as meaningful. If the cat is painful, frail, aggressive under restraint, or impossible to sample safely, tell the clinic. A different device or monitoring design may be safer than escalating conflict.

## Run a caregiver handoff drill

A substitute caregiver should be able to answer five questions without searching private messages:

1. What is the exact current medication and prescribed timing?
2. Where is the clinic’s written missed-dose and poor-appetite plan?
3. What was the last confirmed dose, meal, and notable symptom?
4. Which signs require emergency care, and which clinic is open now?
5. How will the cat travel safely with the log and medication information?

Use a one-page handoff that lists process information, not unnecessary identity data. Keep the medication in its original labeled container, but do not post prescription labels, addresses, phone numbers, account details, or full medical records online. The [emergency foster-care handoff plan](/posts/pet-emergency-foster-care-handoff-plan-2026/) provides a broader continuity structure for unexpected caregiver changes.

## Monthly review: calculate coverage, not control

A simple completeness score can reveal weak operations:

**Coverage percentage = completed required log fields ÷ expected required log fields × 100.**

If the veterinarian asks for five fields daily for fourteen days, there are 70 expected fields. Completing 60 gives 85.7% coverage. That number says only how complete the record is; it does not score the cat’s health or treatment quality. A lower score should prompt simplification—fewer essential fields, easier placement, clearer ownership—not fabricated entries.

At each review, remove obsolete instructions, confirm refill timing, verify emergency contacts, check supply condition, and ask whether the monitoring burden is still clinically useful. Bring the original log rather than a selective summary. Mention sensor gaps, stressful attempts, suspected errors, and deviations instead of hiding them.

## Practical checklist

- Current diagnosis and treatment pathway are written at the top of the log.
- Medication, concentration, measuring device, dose, timing, and meal relationship match the clinic’s current instructions.
- Missed-dose, poor-appetite, vomiting, duplicate-dose, and after-hours actions are explicit.
- One caregiver records each completed dose after it is given.
- Glucose entries include unit, device, time, meal, medication timing, and symptoms.
- The household does not change medication from a single reading.
- SGLT2-treated cats have product-specific ketone and sick-day instructions.
- Emergency transport and substitute-caregiver plans are current.

## FAQ

### Can I change my cat’s insulin after one high or low reading?

No. Sampling, device behavior, stress, food, timing, and clinical condition all affect interpretation. Follow the veterinarian’s written plan and contact the clinic.

### Is a continuous glucose monitor a substitute for veterinary rechecks?

No. It may provide valuable trends, but sensor failure, lag, clinical signs, laboratory findings, diet, weight, and medication type still need professional interpretation.

### What should every caregiver know?

The exact current medication and timing, the missed-dose and poor-appetite instructions, urgent signs, last confirmed dose, and location of the log and emergency contacts.
