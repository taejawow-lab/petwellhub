The myth: cats don't need much water. The reality, drawn from AAFCO nutrient profiles, AAHA fluid therapy guidelines, and Cornell Feline Health Center research: cats need precisely the same hydration as any mammal of comparable size — they're just very bad at recognizing thirst.

This article gives the daily numbers, the dehydration warning signs every owner should know, and the practical setup that veterinary nutritionists actually recommend.

## The 60-80 ml per kg formula

AAFCO and Cornell Feline Health Center publish identical numbers: a healthy cat needs **60-80 ml of water per kg of body weight per day**, total — including moisture from food.

  <figcaption style="text-align: center; font-size: 0.85rem; color: rgb(var(--color-muted-foreground)); margin-top: 0.5rem;">60-80 ml per kg per day, total including food. Wet food covers 70-80% of that.</figcaption>
</figure>

| Cat weight | Daily water needs | If wet food only | If dry food only |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 kg (6.6 lb) | 180-240 ml | ~50 ml from bowl | ~210 ml from bowl |
| 4 kg (8.8 lb) | 240-320 ml | ~70 ml from bowl | ~280 ml from bowl |
| 5 kg (11 lb) | 300-400 ml | ~85 ml from bowl | ~345 ml from bowl |
| 6 kg (13.2 lb) | 360-480 ml | ~100 ml from bowl | ~415 ml from bowl |

The implications are significant. A cat on dry food alone must drink 4-5x more water than one eating wet food. Cornell flags this as the underlying mechanism for why dry-only diets correlate with feline lower urinary tract disease (FLUTD).

## The skin-tent test

AAHA's hydration assessment is the same one veterinarians use in clinics. Gently pinch the skin between the shoulder blades and release.

<div style="background: rgb(var(--color-accent) / 0.08); border: 1px solid rgb(var(--color-accent) / 0.3); padding: 1rem 1.25rem; margin: 1.5rem 0; border-radius: 6px;">
  💡 <strong>How to read the result</strong> — Snaps back instantly = hydrated. Returns in 1-2 seconds = mild dehydration (5%). Tents and stays = moderate to severe (8-12%) — emergency. Older cats may show slight tenting normally; baseline-test your cat when they're confirmed hydrated.
</div>

Other signs from AAHA + Cornell:

- **Gum check** — Healthy gums are moist and pink. Tacky, sticky, or pale gums signal dehydration.
- **Capillary refill** — Press the gum, release. Color should return in under 2 seconds.
- **Eye position** — Sunken eyes are advanced dehydration.
- **Behavior** — Lethargy, decreased grooming, decreased appetite combined with above signs warrant a vet visit.

## Why cats are bad at drinking

<figure style="margin: 1.5rem 0;">
  <img src="/images/illustrations/post1-bowl.png" alt="Watercolor illustration of cat food bowl beside fresh cucumber and water glass" style="width: 100%; border-radius: 8px; aspect-ratio: 1/1; object-fit: cover;" loading="lazy" />
  <figcaption style="text-align: center; font-size: 0.85rem; color: rgb(var(--color-muted-foreground)); margin-top: 0.5rem;">Cats evolved to get water from prey. Their thirst response is intentionally subtle.</figcaption>
</figure>

Cornell Feline Health Center on the evolutionary background: domestic cats descend from *Felis lybica* (African wildcat), an obligate carnivore from arid regions. Their kidneys evolved to be exceptionally efficient at concentrating urine, and their thirst drive evolved to be triggered late — only when the body is already several percent dehydrated.

In wild conditions this works because mouse prey is roughly 70% water. In domestic conditions on dry kibble, the ancestral system fails. The cat doesn't feel thirsty until well past optimal hydration.

Cats also prefer water away from food bowls — likely another evolutionary holdover (predators don't drink from contaminated kill sites in the wild).

## The wet vs. dry decision

This is where the science is clearest. From AAFCO, AAHA, WSAVA:

- **Wet food (canned/pouch)** — 70-80% moisture. Provides most daily water needs. Recommended primary or 50%+ of diet for healthy cats; mandatory for cats with history of urinary issues.
- **Dry food (kibble)** — 6-10% moisture. Convenient and less expensive but requires the cat to actively compensate by drinking. Many cats don't.
- **Mixed feeding** — A wet/dry combination (50/50 or 70/30 wet) gives the moisture benefit with kibble's convenience for free-feeding.

</figure>

Beyond the skin-tent test, a hydrated cat shows:

- Clear, alert eyes (not sunken or dull)
- Glossy, smooth coat (not dry or brittle)
- Pink, moist gums
- Normal urination — 2-4 times daily, light yellow color
- Normal appetite and activity level

If urination drops below 2 times daily, gums turn dry, or coat appearance dulls, schedule a vet visit. Especially for older cats, kidney disease and diabetes (both common in cats over 10) can present first as hydration issues.

## The practical setup veterinary nutritionists recommend

From Cornell Feline Health, AAHA, and WSAVA combined:

1. **Multiple water stations** — One per cat, plus one extra. Different rooms.
2. **Away from food** — Minimum 1-2 meters from food bowls.
3. **Wide ceramic or stainless** — Avoid plastic.
4. **Refresh daily** — Cats refuse stagnant water faster than dogs.
5. **One fountain in the mix** — If your cat is drinking less than expected, a fountain often dramatically improves intake.
6. **Wet food primary** — Aim for 50%+ of calories from wet food. Mandatory for cats with kidney or urinary history.

This is not optimization — these are the baseline recommendations from the three primary feline nutrition authorities in North America. Following this setup correlates with documented reductions in FLUTD, kidney issues, and dehydration episodes in domestic cats over 10 years of age.
